# Social Trust, Institution, and Economic Growth
This article examines the relationship between social trust, formal institutions, and economic growth across Chinese provinces from 2001-2009. The research finds that social trust significantly promotes economic growth, with effects that vary by institutional strength. At lower institutional levels, social trust has a particularly strong positive impact on growth (coefficient of 13.8), but this effect weakens as institutional quality improves. The study also demonstrates that stronger formal institutions boost economic growth through reduced transaction costs and improved resource allocation. Importantly, the combined effects of social trust and institutions are most powerful—when both are strengthened together, they create synergistic benefits for economic development. The authors conclude that policymakers should focus on developing informal institutions (trust and social capital) alongside formal institutional reforms to promote sustainable economic growth, particularly in less-developed regions of China.
# Networking in Weak Institutions: When Is It Good for Small Business Investment? The Case of Vietnam 
This study investigates how social networks influence small business investment decisions in Vietnam, with a focus on understanding when networking is beneficial given weak institutional environments. The research examines three types of networks: business-specific (connections with businesspeople in the same sector), bank-specific (ties with bank officials), and political-specific (connections with government officials and politicians). Using data from over 9,800 firm-year observations from 2005-2015, the authors find that social networks can effectively substitute for formal institutions by helping entrepreneurs overcome institutional weaknesses in legal enforcement, corruption, bureaucratic compliance, and governance transparency.

The key finding is that political-specific networks are the most effective at moderating the negative effects of weak institutions on firm investment. While business-specific networks help with market information and opportunities, and bank-specific networks facilitate access to financing, only political connections enable firms to navigate systemic institutional voids. The study demonstrates that networking is particularly valuable in regions with lower institutional quality, where entrepreneurs leverage guanxi (quasi-familial relationships) and personal connections to secure resources, reduce transaction costs, and gain favorable treatment from officials. The authors conclude that policymakers should recognize social networks as legitimate informal institutions that complement formal governance reforms, especially in developing economies with incomplete institutional frameworks.
